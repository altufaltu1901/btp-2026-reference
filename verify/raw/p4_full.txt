Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as potential clinical decision-making tools to aid MDTs. Our aim was to compare different LLM-generated treatment recommendations for HCC cases with MDT decisions. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed 100 HCC cases discussed during MDT meetings in a tertiary-care hospital in Cluj-Napoca, Romania. Identical prompts and structured clinical information were offered to four different LLMs (ChatGPT-5, a customized Tumor Board ChatGPT, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Gemini 2.5 Pro), each of which was required to provide treatment recommendations. Concordance with the MDT decisions was assessed across the first, second, and third treatment options. Inter-model differences were evaluated using Cochran’s Q and Holm-adjusted exact McNemar tests. Chance-corrected agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ), and generalized estimating equations evaluated associations between concordance and clinical complexity. Results: First-recommendation concordance was 81% (95% CI 72.2–87.5) for GPT-5, 80% (71.1–86.7) for Gemini 2.5 Pro, 78% (68.9–85.0) for TumorBoard ChatGPT, and 63% (53.2–71.8) for Gemini 2.5 Flash; cumulative top-3 concordance reached 95%, 92%, 95%, and 86%, respectively. GPT-5 (κ = 0.754) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (κ = 0.743) showed substantial chance-corrected agreement. Gemini 2.5 Flash showed significantly lower concordance, including after adjustment for BCLC stage, Child–Pugh class, tumor burden, and treatment category (p = 0.006), while these clinical complexity variables were not significantly associated with concordance. Conclusions: LLMs showed moderate-to-substantial agreement with MDT decisions, with meaningful inter-model differences, for a complex disease, such as HCC. Our study highlights that, while the need for human oversight is essential, LLMs could serve as supportive tools for expert guidance in some clinical settings and underscore AI’s potential in enhancing liver cancer care.\nKeywords: hepatocellular carcinoma; clinical decision support; artificial intelligence; multidisciplinary tumor boards; ChatGPT; Gemini; concordance analysis\n1. Introduction\nLiver cancer is one of the leading types of cancer worldwide and is currently the third most common cause of cancer-related mortality, with hepatocellular carcinoma accounting for approximately 90% of primary liver cancers [1]. Although the incidence of HCC has declined in certain regions, emerging data indicate a shift in etiological risk factors from virus-related liver disease toward alcohol-associated liver disease and metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease [2]. This epidemiological transition has important implications across the entire spectrum of liver cancer care, from prevention strategies and surveillance to treatment allocation and survival outcomes [3].\nThe complexity of therapeutic decision-making in HCC is guided by three interacting determinants: liver function, tumor burden, and cancer-related symptoms, and is broadly structured along the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system [3]. However, different therapies and individual clinical scenarios often lead to demanding exercises for clinical decision-making. In this context, clinical guidelines recommend that a coordinated multidisciplinary team comprising a hepatologist, hepatobiliary and/or transplant surgeon, oncologist, radiation and interventional radiologist, pathologist, specialized nurse, palliative care specialist, and dietitian should ensure cohesive and effective treatment allocation that is patient-centered, with minimal therapeutic ambiguity [4,5]. MDT care is associated with improved overall survival, higher adherence to guidelines, and enhanced patient satisfaction, with studies demonstrating a decrease in time to treatment and reduced mortality [6,7,8].\nAlthough MDT care is recognized as a vital component of the healthcare system, there are also factors that hinder its productivity and compromise the desired outcome [8]. Coordinating different specialists, inconsistent documentation, high clinical workload, and inter-specialist ambiguity can negatively affect patient benefits [9]. Artificial intelligence (AI), through various systems, has the potential to improve liver cancer care, from early detection to personalized treatment plans [10,11], and it seems that AI can also provide support tools that improve, but not replace, the role of MDTs [12]. Early evaluations indicate that AI-based systems demonstrate a high degree of alignment with expert multidisciplinary tumor board decisions while potentially reducing the cognitive workload [13]. Reported concordance between AI-generated recommendations and MDT decisions across multiple cancer types typically ranges from 70% to 90% [14].\nLarge language models (LLMs), also known as the core engines typically powering chatbots, are at the intersection of natural language processing and deep learning in the field of AI, and based on their ability to access and structure large datasets in a short amount of time, they have generated enthusiasm for their potential to assist the MDT decision-making process [15]. Among them, the Chat Generative Pre-trained Transformer (ChatGPT), developed by OpenAI (OpenAI, LLC, San Francisco, CA, USA), and Google’s artificial intelligence platform (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA), now known as Gemini, are among the most widely used systems. Since their introduction in 2022, their use for medical purposes has increased exponentially [16]. Several studies have evaluated the concordance between LLM-generated recommendations and MDT decisions across different types of neoplasia, such as gastric cancer, pancreatic cancer, HCC, cholangiocarcinoma, and head and neck cancers. These investigations generally report moderate to high agreement in standardized or early-stage scenarios, with greater discordance observed in complex cases that require nuanced multidisciplinary judgment [16,17,18,19,20,21].\nAgainst this background, our study aimed to systematically analyze the concordance rate between four LLMs—ChatGPT-5, a customized ChatGPT model named TumorBoard ChatGPT, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Gemini 2.5 Pro—and MDT treatment recommendations for 100 successive HCC cases, when provided with the same structured information. Using the MDT recommendations as a reference point, we sought to quantify agreement rates and visualize patterns of agreement and disagreement between the LLMs and MDT. Despite the growing body of evidence on LLM performance in oncology, existing studies have predominantly evaluated single models across heterogeneous cancer populations, and no study has yet simultaneously compared multiple contemporaneous LLMs—including the most recently released generation of models—against MDT decisions in a disease-specific HCC cohort spanning the full spectrum of BCLC stages and treatment modalities. Addressing these gaps is clinically relevant given the inherent decision complexity of HCC treatment algorithms and the uneven global accessibility of expert multidisciplinary care. In a context where decisions regarding HCC treatment are highly multimodal, our pilot study aimed to explore patterns of concordance between large language models and a single institutional MDT. This exploratory analysis does not assume that MDT decisions represent an absolute gold standard but rather uses them as a pragmatic real-world comparator to quantify agreement patterns, revealing the potential role of LLMs in enhancing multidisciplinary workflow efficiency. Consequently, concordance should be interpreted as agreement with local multidisciplinary decision-making rather than evidence of treatment correctness.\n2. Materials and Methods\nThis retrospective study included 100 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma who were previously discussed during MDT meetings, between 2020 and 2025, at the Regional Institute of Gastroenterology and Hepatology, ‘Prof. Dr. Octavian Fodor’, Cluj-Napoca, Romania. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (No. 9653/11 August 2025). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the anonymization of patient data, informed consent was waived.\nThe multidisciplinary tumor board team consisted of two hepatologists, two hepatobiliary surgeons, one transplant surgeon, two oncologists, one pathologist, an interventional radiologist, one radiation radiologist, and one specialized nurse. Patient clinical data were presented in a structured format a week prior to the official MDT meeting. Imaging studies were re-evaluated in real time by specialized radiologists with extensive expertise in HCC diagnosis. Following the discussion, a consensus decision was reached, along with two or three more alternatives that could be suitable for the patient (when possible), in case the primary decision was not feasible for any reason. Treatment recommendations were based on multidisciplinary consensus and were informed by contemporary BCLC, European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), and American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) clinical practice guidelines, while also incorporating patient-specific factors, institutional expertise, and local treatment availability.\n2.1. Patient Cohort\nA total of 100 case files of patients aged > 18 years with an established HCC diagnosis were included in the study. Patients with indefinite diagnoses, or multiple neoplasias in addition to HCC, were excluded before being presented in MDT meetings. All 100 consecutive HCC cases presented to the MDT met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis, with no subsequent exclusions or missing clinical data requiring imputation.\n2.2. LLMs—Prompt Formatting and Data Evaluation\nThe clinical data for each patient were introduced in a Microsoft Word document that was identical to that presented during the MDT meetings without any identifying information (all direct patient identifiers, including names, initials, dates of birth, patient/hospital identification numbers, and contact information, were removed). The documents were compiled by a physician who did not attend the board meeting or have knowledge of the treatment decisions, and contained patient demographics (gender and age), laboratory values, imaging results, and information on prior treatments.\nA prompt was initially developed and given to the four LLMs each time a new case was presented (Supplementary File), along with a Microsoft Word document that contained clinical data. The choice of treatment offered by each LLM was recorded as the primary choice, along with the second and third options for each case. All LLMs were accessed from online interfaces. Using web-based consumer interfaces, the LLM evaluations were performed between 1 August 2025 and 1 September 2025. The models were accessed through ChatGPT Plus Subscription and Google AI Pro Subscription; the GPT-5 Thinking model was used, being available at the time. The customized TumorBoard ChatGPT was accessed through the same ChatGPT Plus environment and was developed using the Custom GPT functionality. Web browsing/search and ChatGPT memory were not disabled, each case being evaluated in an independent chat. Each patient case was submitted once to each LLM, and no repeated inferences were performed. Because the models were not accessed through application programming interfaces, inference parameters such as temperature, top-p sampling, and other generation settings were not available to users and therefore could not be standardized or reported. Each case was evaluated in a newly initiated and independent chat session to minimize contextual carryover between patients. No patient-specific information from previous cases was available within the active conversation context when a new case was submitted.\nThe choice of model used—ChatGPT-5 (GPT-5—OpenAI, accessed through the ChatGPT interface), Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Gemini 2.5 Pro—was based on the most recent publicly available model at the time of the analysis. GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro were included as flagship models from two leading AI developers, Gemini 2.5 Flash was selected to evaluate a lightweight, speed-optimized model, and TumorBoard ChatGPT was included to assess whether domain-specific prompt customization could improve agreement with MDT recommendations compared with the general-purpose GPT-5 model. TumorBoard ChatGPT was developed using the Custom GPT functionality available through the ChatGPT platform. The customization consisted of persistent instructions designed to simulate a multidisciplinary tumor board discussion and encourage guideline-based treatment allocation. No additional model training, fine-tuning, modification of model weights, or external training datasets were used. Radiological examinations were not provided as original CT, MRI, or CEUS images. Instead, standardized text summaries extracted from the radiology reports, including clinically relevant imaging characteristics (e.g., LI-RADS category, lesion number, maximum diameter, lesion location, vascular invasion, and extrahepatic disease, where applicable), were incorporated into the clinical case description presented to each LLM.\nThe overall experimental workflow consisted of the following steps: (1) selection of eligible HCC cases discussed by the MDT; (2) anonymization and preparation of standardized clinical summaries, including imaging report summaries; (3) submission of identical prompts and case summaries to each LLM in independent chat sessions; (4) recording of the first, second, and third treatment recommendations; top-2 and top-3 concordance was assessed cumulatively, indicating that the MDT treatment decision was included among the first two or first three LLM recommendations, respectively; (5) comparison with MDT decisions using concordance analyses including 95% confidence intervals and chance-corrected agreement using Cohen’s kappa; (6) comparative and exploratory analyses, including paired inter-model comparisons using Cochran’s Q test followed by exact McNemar tests with Holm correction, treatment-specific sensitivity and precision analyses, and generalized estimating equations (GEE) to evaluate concordance while accounting for clinical complexity.\nConcordance was defined as agreement between the MDT treatment category and the corresponding treatment recommendation generated by the large language model.\n2.3. Statistical Analysis\nCategorical data are presented as counts, percentages, and bar plots, whereas quantitative data are presented as medians and interquartile ranges. The assessment of MDT recommendations versus the LLM recommendations was presented using Sankey plots.\nConcordance of the models’ suggestions with tumor board decisions was expressed as percentages with 95% Wilson confidence intervals. Comparisons between the models as paired binary outcomes were tested with Cochran’s Q test, for each recommendation rank, and followed by post hoc pairwise exact McNemar tests. These tests were adjusted for multiple comparisons with the Holm–Bonferroni correction.\nCohen’s kappa values with 95% confidence intervals were used to compute chance-corrected agreement of the models with tumor board decisions, due to the fact raw percentage agreement can be inflated if the categories are not uniformly distributed. Rare treatment subtypes were grouped for sensitivity analysis. For each treatment category, the confusion matrices, with sensitivity and precision, were computed for each model compared with the tumor board decision.\nA generalized estimating equation model was used to assess if the performance was different in relative to case complexity. The model had, as the outcome, the concordance (as a binary variable), with model identity, BCLC stage, Child–Pugh class, tumor burden (Milan criteria) and treatment category as independent variables. Clustering was used on a case basis.\nAll statistical analyses were performed in the R environment for statistical computing and graphics (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), version 4.5.2.\n3. Results\nA total of 100 patients with an established HCC diagnosis who were evaluated in MDT meetings between 2020 and 2025 were included. The clinical data of the patients are presented in Table 1. The majority of patients were male (74%), with most patients presenting with a preserved performance status (77% classified as ECOG 0) and preserved liver function (73% of patients classified as Child–Pugh A). Regarding the most common etiologies, alcohol-associated liver disease (43%) and chronic hepatitis C infection (35%) were predominant, followed by hepatitis B (14%) and metabolic liver disease (5%).\nTable 1.\nPatient characteristics.\nAccording to BCLC staging, most patients were classified as early or intermediate stage, with 6% in stage 0, 56% in stage A, and 25% in stage B. Advanced and terminal stages were less frequent, with 10% and 3% of patients classified as BCLC C and D, respectively.\nThe median alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) level was 19.4 ng/mL (IQR 5.9–109.1). The median liver stiffness measurement was 27.5 kPa (IQR 18.3–36.9), and the median hepatic venous pressure gradient was 12 mmHg (IQR, 8.5–17).\nImaging assessments most frequently involved computed tomography (33%) alone or in combination with contrast-enhanced ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging. The majority of lesions were classified as LI-RADS 5 (98%). Most patients had a single tumor nodule (60%), and the median largest tumor diameter was 34 mm (IQR, 22–50).\n3.1. MDT Treatment Allocation\nFollowing multidisciplinary tumor board discussions, treatment recommendations reflected the heterogeneity of the disease stage and liver function. Curative-intent therapies, including surgical resection (25%) and ablation (30%), account for a substantial proportion of decisions. Locoregional therapies, primarily transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), alone or in combination with other modalities, were recommended in 15% of cases. Liver transplantation, either as a primary strategy or with bridging therapy, was recommended in 14% of cases. Systemic therapy was recommended in 13% of patients, either alone or in combination with transplant considerations, whereas best supportive care was advised in 7% of patients.\nThe concordance rates of the four LLMs compared with the MDT treatment decisions across their first, second, and third recommendation options are shown in Figure 1 and Table 2. For the first treatment recommendation, ChatGPT-5 achieved a success rate of 81%, followed by Gemini 2.5 Pro at 80% concordance and customized TumorBoard ChatGPT at 78%. The lowest initial agreement of 63% was recorded for Gemini Flash. For the second therapeutic option, all models presented with increased concordance rates, with Gemini Pro 2.5 taking the lead with a success rate of 93%. For the third option, overall concordance with MDT recommendations was highest for all LLMs, with ChatGPT-5 and TumorBoard ChatGPT both achieving a concordance rate of 95%, Gemini Pro 2.5 92%, and Gemini Flash 86%.\nFigure 1. Paired Concordance Test\nThe concordance of each LLM recommendation with the tumor board decisions was assessed for each model for each recommendation rank in Table 2. We performed a test of concordance for all models, taking into account the correlations between concordances with the Cochran’s Q test, and found significant differences for all recommendation ranks. Then, we performed post hoc pair-wise exact tests (corrected for multiple comparison with a Hom–Bonferroni adjustment) (Supplementary Table S1). Gemini Flash was statistically significantly different from the other models for the first and second recommendations. No statistically significant differences were found between ChatGPT 5, TumorBoard ChatGPT and Gemini 2.5 Pro, no matter the rank of recommendation. For the third recommendation, although the overall test outcomes were significant, the post hoc comparisons did not stay significant. Thus, Gemini Flash had lower concordance with the tumor board compared to the other models. No significant difference was observed between ChatGPT and TumorBoard ChatGPT.\n3.3. The chance-corrected agreement sustained the observations made in the previous concordance analysis: ChatGPT (κ = 0.754) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (κ = 0.743) had substantial agreement with the tumor board, while TumorBoard ChatGPT (κ = 0.622) and Gemini Flash (κ = 0.537) had moderate agreement.\nTable 3.\nCohen’s kappa (chance-corrected agreement) with 95% CI, granular and collapsed category schemes.\nTable 4.\nSensitivity and precision reported per treatment category (with a sufficient number of cases).\nA more detailed analysis looking at what happened per treatment category is shown in Table 3. All models had low precision for TACE (positive predictive value) (from 0.280 to 0.778), that is, the proportion of cases where each model also indicated TACE when the tumor board had recommended TACE. This means that the models over-recommended TACE in comparison with tumor board decisions. The TumorBoard ChatGPT and Gemini Flash had problems in recommending surgical resection (sensitivity of 0.577), missing an important percentage that the tumor board had recommended, compared to the ChatGPT (0.885), and Gemini Pro (0.808) models. Performance Stratified by Clinical Complexity\nTo assess if concordance differed as a result of case clinical complexity, we used a generalized estimating equation model that predicted the concordance outcome (as a binary variable for the first recommendation), adjusting for the model, BCLC stage, Child–Pugh class, tumor burden, and treatment category (Table 5). Gemini Flash was significantly less concordant with tumor board decisions than the reference model, a result similar to the previous ones, in unadjusted analyses. ChatGPT 5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro were not statistically significantly different compared with TumorBoard ChatGPT. BCLC stage, Child–Pugh class, tumor burden category, and treatment category were not significantly associated with the concordance, although Child–Pugh class B showed a slight trend toward lower concordance compared to class A. Overall, the results of the analysis suggest that the differences are due to the models and not to the case complexity.\nTable 5.\nGeneralized estimating equation model of first-recommendation concordance, adjusted for clinical complexity (BCLC stage, Child–Pugh class, tumor burden, and treatment category) and the model.\n3.5. Discussion\nGiven the intricacy of HCC as a disease, a direct link between disease stage and specific treatment is often lacking, which is why MDT input is essential. Because of the board’s human nature, certain biases can hinder the allocation of a specific therapy to a given patient. For example, excessive empiricism or rationalism, loss aversion, or a lack of clustered principles [4] are all potentially misleading and can lead to cognitive distortions that could influence treatment selection, even among highly experienced clinicians. This is where artificial intelligence may intervene, potentially improving decision-making consistency by eliminating idiosyncratic decision variation, minimizing cognitive biases, and reducing the overall errors caused by lapses in human judgment [13].\nA consistent number of studies have highlighted the potential role of different LLMs in oncology practice, particularly the role of ChatGPT, with Gemini being less used for this type of research. In a study involving 610 cancer patients who were presented at MDT meetings over the course of 3 years, ChatGPT-4 revealed a high level of agreement between its predictions and tumor board decisions, with major compatibility (i.e., a score of 4) being achieved in 67.5% of the cases, stressing the model’s capabilities in reproducing expert clinical judgment [22]. Discrepancies mostly involved complex cancer cases, but they were present in only a minority of cases. While Erdat et al. [22] used a large heterogeneous cancer cohort and a structured compatibility scale with independent clinical raters to assess the compatibility between ChatGPT and tumor board decisions, our study focused on only one type of cancer and compared multiple LLMs (ChatGPT, customized ChatGPT, Gemini Flash, and Gemini Pro) directly with MDT decisions. Aghamaliyev et al. [17] evaluated the concordance between ChatGPT-3.5 treatment recommendations and multidisciplinary tumor board decisions across a series of 115 patients with gastrointestinal malignancies, and reported that while the model aligned with overall treatment strategy in 83% of the cases, exact regimen concordance was lower (65% concordance rate), and cases involving individualized chemotherapy were particularly challenging. Similarly to our findings, their analysis, which used descriptive concordance rates expressed as percentages, highlights that LLMs can reproduce general oncologic reasoning but struggle with precise, case-specific decisions. However, our study differs in scope and granularity, focusing on a disease-specific cohort (hepatocellular carcinoma) with quantifiable first-attempt concordance and iterative refinement across multiple LLMs. These concordance rates reflect numerical agreement with MDT decisions and should not be interpreted as a measure of pertinence to treatment, guideline conformity, or patient outcome benefits. Appropriately, the 81% concordance rate should be regarded as a measure of agreement rather than clinical significance; whether this degree of concordance translates into improved patient outcomes, treatment appropriateness, or decision quality remains unknown and requires prospective validation studies incorporating clinical endpoints. Standardized clinical summaries captured the key clinical variables, but qualitative imaging assessment, technical feasibility, patient preferences, and institutional considerations were not fully represented and may have contributed to the observed discordances between LLM and MDT recommendations. Additionally, although GPT-5 demonstrated the numerically greatest first-choice concordance, the present exploratory study was not designed or statistically powered to establish the superiority of one LLM over another; therefore, inter-model differences should be interpreted descriptively rather than inferentially.\nA study by Sorin et al. [23] revealed that ChatGPT-3.5 can act as a reliable decision support tool for breast cancer patients, having a 70% case decision compatibility with a tumor board, although the small sample size of 10 patients limits the generalizability of the results; in contrast, Griewing et al. [24] reached a 58.8% concordance between ChatGPT-3.5 and a tumor board involving 20 patients with breast cancer, concluding that LLMs are not inadequate support tools for tumor boards. Their findings are based on the older version of ChatGPT, so the results must be interpreted cautiously. A two-year retrospective study of 98 oncologic cases, led by Hernández-Flores et al. [16], showed moderate agreement between ChatGPT 4.0, Gemini Flash 1.5 and a multidisciplinary board regarding decisions such as surgery (Gemini: κ  =  0.194, p  =  0.046; ChatGPT 4o: κ  =  0.467, p   ≤  0.001) and radiotherapy (Gemini: κ  =  0.214, p  =  0.012; ChatGPT 4.0: κ  =  0.525, p   ≤  0.001), with none to slight agreement for imaging studies (Gemini: κ  =  0.100, p  =  0.087; ChatGPT 4.0: κ  =  0.024, p  =  0.592) and chemotherapy (Gemini: κ  =  0.089, p  =  0.316; ChatGPT 4.0: κ  =  0.336, p  =  0.001). Compared with Hernández-Flores et al., our study evaluated a higher-order clinical task—precise therapy selection in HCC—rather than a binary treatment indication, enabling a more stringent and disease-specific assessment of LLM–MDT concordance. Lechien et al. used 20 head and neck cancer cases that were presented to ChatGPT-4; it was asked to analyze them, suggesting additional examinations and therapeutic approaches. The study concluded that therapy decisions were consistent with the tumor board in 13 cases (65%); the LLM provided adequate explanations for 19 cases (95%) and proposed a significantly higher and correct number of examinations than the clinicians (103 versus 72; p  =  0.001). Similarly to their analysis, our HCC-focused analysis demonstrates that large language models can align with expert panel decisions in a meaningful subset of cases. However, unlike their study, where consistency was assessed primarily through predefined consistency metrics (e.g., Kendall tau) and basic treatment adequacy, our study explicitly quantified first-attempt concordance across multiple distinct therapeutic categories (surgery, ablation, systemic treatment, etc.). Additionally, our incorporation of Sankey-based tracking of treatment transitions portrays a picture of variability across different LLMs.\nComplex clinical decision-making systems, such as in case with hepatocellular carcinoma, often display skewed distributions of influence, whereby a restricted subset of variables accounts for a disproportionate share of treatment decision outcomes, a pattern abstractly aligned with Pareto’s principle (i.e., 80% of outcomes caused by 20% of factors). For HCC, this principle applies due to the small number of dominant factors (tumor stage, liver function, and performance status), while other variables only provide contextual alignment. This structure may partly explain why pattern-recognition systems, including large language models, can generate plausible treatment recommendations in many standardized scenarios, while still diverging in more nuanced cases requiring multidisciplinary judgment. In our study, most discrepancies between LLM treatment allocation and MDT decisions occurred because of non-algorithmic but guideline-supported recommendations or subtle clinical constraints. For example, in early-stage HCC (BCLC stages 0 and A), guidelines suggest either surgery or ablation, depending on the three pillars already mentioned that dictate most decisions, but also considering the surgical risk (extent of hepatectomy, expected volume of the remnant liver), patient’s comorbidities, and institutional expertise for both types of treatment. We found that both ChatGPT and Gemini considered clinically significant portal hypertension (HVPG > 10 mmHg) and Child–Pugh Class B cirrhosis to be contraindications to surgery; however, in real-life settings, they are not absolute contraindications if the surgery risk is minimal, which aligns with guideline recommendations [5]. In other cases, they considered surgery feasible without considering factors that categorized the patient as high-risk; this aligns with LLMs’ limitations regarding the integration of non-binary clinical suitability constraints. Another pattern observed in LLMs was the preference for TACE for intermediate-advanced cases (BCLC B), without acknowledging certain clinical aspects that can be considered contraindications for treatment, such as significant liver dysfunction, portal vein thrombosis, and large tumor burden [25]. There is a lack of standardized cutoffs that preclude TACE treatment, meaning that the choice regarding patient suitability remains for the MDT and is highly variable depending on the center availability and expertise; understandably, LLMs struggle with clinical concepts that are qualitative and longitudinal [26].\nAssessing the medical accuracy of LLM-based chatbots is essential for determining the effectiveness and safety of this promising technology before it can be incorporated into clinical practice [27]. A phenomenon known as ‘hallucinations’ is a frequent encounter for these types of chatbots, with their recommendations in these scenarios spanning from innocent behavior, such as inventing references, to dangerous actions, like recommending harmful treatment [28]. In our analysis, we did not encounter this type of behavior, since a prompt was given ahead of each case discussion, in which the chatbots were instructed to perform under certain conditions. We cannot generalize our findings to real-world situations, where, for example, patients would directly access and ask for medical advice, which is why it is imperative that the output given by LLMs is verified by a human professional. Regarding inter-model variability, differences in fine-tuning strategies, such as domain adaptation, ref. [29] could partially explain the results of our analysis. The mechanisms by which LLMs interpret medical terminology, apply staging systems, and select guideline-supported therapeutic options remain incompletely understood. Differences in retrieval and tool-integration mechanisms may further contribute to inter-model variability [29]. Although we aimed to standardize model behavior through structured prompt and identical case inputs, the internal processes by which each model retrieves, prioritizes, or synthesizes guideline information are not externally controllable and may differ across systems.\nThe increased concordance observed when considering the second and third treatment recommendations reflects the presence of multiple guideline-supported therapeutic options within each BCLC stage. Consequently, even when the first-ranked recommendation differed from the MDT decision, alternative LLM suggestions frequently remained within the appropriate therapeutic spectrum. This phenomenon likely contributes to the higher concordance rates compared to the first-choice agreement. The purpose of this study was not to determine whether LLMs generated the objectively correct treatment recommendation, as such a gold standard does not exist for many complex HCC scenarios. Instead, the analysis evaluated the extent to which LLM recommendations aligned with multidisciplinary expert consensus generated in routine clinical practice. A formal case-level analysis of discordant recommendations across LLMs was also not performed, limiting the ability to identify systematic inter-model biases or recurrent disagreement patterns Accordingly, the multi-model comparison presented here is descriptive and visual in nature, and inter-model differences should be interpreted as observational patterns rather than as statistically tested conclusions.\nIt is imperative to mention that redacted imaging summaries (e.g., LI-RADS category, tumor size, and number of nodules) were provided, as text-based LLMs are not trained to process imaging features. Future multimodal LLMs capable of directly processing radiological images in conjunction with structured clinical information may further improve treatment recommendations by incorporating imaging-derived features and spatial relationships that cannot be fully conveyed through textual summaries alone. Overall, the analysis is consistent with the clinical decision-making model, where staging and treatment decisions depend on multiple physiological and tumor factors.\nAlthough the importance of MDT for complex cancer cases, such as hepatocellular carcinoma, has already been established, the accessibility of a formal tumor board is uneven across the healthcare system. There is a high degree of disparity among healthcare providers and institutions, with remote populations facing geographic and logistical barriers that preclude participation in multidisciplinary discussions, motivating interest in virtual tumor boards and other supportive technologies to improve equity in cancer care [30]. For hospitals that reside in limited resource settings, access to experts in hepatobiliary/transplant surgery or interventional ultrasound/radiology, which are essential in this type of MDT, is scarce, which can result in critical delays or suboptimal treatments. Projections indicate that by 2030, most cancer deaths will occur in low- and middle-income countries, emphasizing the urgency of narrowing inequities in cancer care worldwide [31]. In this context, our analysis addresses scenarios where AI-generated recommendations can approximate expert consensus at a high level, offering a clinical support tool that cannot and should not replace MDT expertise but may offer a guideline-supported second opinion that minimizes decisional uncertainty and can improve quality of care.\nNotably, the recent introduction of the BCLC-CUSE (Complexity, Uncertainty, Subjectivity, and Emotion) framework [3] and the associated BCLC.CUSE.AI platform highlights the growing interest in structured, AI-assisted support for multidisciplinary decision-making in HCC. BCLC.CUSE.AI is under development, and its purpose is to offer a guideline-anchored appraisal of evidence that will be widely available and should reduce the tumor board workload. In this context, our study provides important empirical evidence on how LLMs align with MDT decisions, while also identifying areas of discordance.\nAdditionally, in resource-limited settings, LLMs could potentially serve as a first-pass triage tool for guideline-straightforward early-stage cases, while automatically flagging higher-complexity cases for expert review, such as training the models to recognize red flag clinical data, using multiple models and flagging disagreements, or assigning a complexity-based scoring system with points allocated for each data input, with automatic escalation of flagged cases to specialists or MDT reviews. Ultimately, a collaborative approach is needed, as human input is essential to ensure that performance is safe and consistent.\nOur study had several limitations that warrant consideration. This was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size, which limits the generalizability of the results. The MDT decisions, which were used as reference points to identify LLM correspondence, reflect evaluations by experts from a single center within a specific institutional context; their decisions do not fully apprehend the variability across centers. Concordance with this MDT, therefore, represents agreement with local expert opinion rather than validated clinical correctness. Variability in institutional practices may influence therapeutic selection, even within established guideline frameworks; accordingly, the results quantify alignment patterns rather than validate the clinical superiority or safety of LLM-generated recommendations. Patient outcomes were not included in our study; therefore, we cannot directly infer the consequences of LLM decisions regarding the clinical benefits, safety, or superiority of LLM-generated recommendations. Furthermore, the structured ranking of the first, second, and third recommendations simplifies the dynamic nature of MDT discussions and may overestimate the agreement when alternative guideline-supported strategies overlap. The study cohort also included only radiologically unequivocal HCC cases (98% LI-RADS 5), which can potentially inflate the concordance analysis by excluding diagnostically ambiguous cases for which MDT input is essential. Patients with concurrent malignancies were also excluded to reduce confounding in treatment allocation and to focus the analysis on HCC-specific decision-making. However, because multiple primary malignancies may occur in clinical practice, this exclusion criterion may limit the generalizability of the findings to more complex real-world oncology populations. As patients with concurrent malignancies typically present more complex decision scenarios involving competing oncologic priorities and systemic treatment interactions, their exclusion most plausibly results in a modest overestimation of the reported concordance rates relative to unselected real-world populations. Each case was evaluated only once by each LLM; therefore, within-model response variability and output stability across repeated inferences were not assessed. Furthermore, only the final treatment recommendation was recorded for each LLM, without capturing or evaluating the underlying reasoning narrative; structured assessment of guideline adherence, staging logic, and contraindication recognition within LLM outputs represents an essential component of future studies prior to any consideration of clinical implementation. Finally, unlike previous studies, which mostly employed independent clinical evaluators, our analysis relied on a direct categorical comparison between LLM and MDT decisions, which precludes an in-depth analysis of discordant LLM outputs. Future studies should therefore incorporate multicenter cohorts and multi-institutional expert consensus panels to provide a more robust comparative reference and account for inter-center variability in HCC treatment allocation.\n5. Conclusions\nOur results indicate that certain LLMs, ChatGPT and Gemini 2.5 Pro in particular, generated treatment recommendations in substantial agreement with the multidisciplinary tumor board, both in raw concordance and after correction for chance agreement, supporting their potential use as supportive tools in selected contexts. However, performance was not similar between models: one LLM (Gemini Flash) showed significantly and consistently lower concordance in all performed analyses. Treatment type analysis further identified specific error patterns, for example, a tendency across all models to over-recommend TACE relative to the tumor board’s real decisions, and, for one LLM, reduced sensitivity for surgical resection, indicating that model errors cluster in particular treatment categories rather than occurring at random. Given the small sample size, the analysis did not encompass the full spectrum of clinical factors considered in MDT meetings and did not explain LLM decisions, which would be imperative before integrating an LLM into clinical work. Based on our results, the use of LLMs for treatment without human supervision is not recommended, and model choice, treatment-specific error patterns, and case complexity should be considered when evaluating any future role for these tools.
